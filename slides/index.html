2 · The crux

Pretraining is next-token prediction at scale

Predict the next token, trillions of times. To win, the model must absorb grammar, facts, reasoning, code and style. Nobody teaches it the rules.

Consequence 1

Knows a lot, obeys nothing

A raw base model just continues text. Assistant behaviour comes later, from instruction tuning.

Consequence 2

Frozen at the cutoff

Anything newer, or private to you, is missing. That gap is exactly what RAG fills.

Culinary school: cook everything once and absorb the grammar of cooking, but know nothing about your restaurant.

The simple objective applied at colossal scale is the whole magic. Capability is forced out by the pressure to predict. The two consequences set up the rest of the session: obedience comes from fine-tuning (instruction tuning is itself fine-tuning), fresh facts come from RAG.

2 · Base vs instruct

Same weights at birth, different habits

Same prompt to both: “Classify this ticket and reply with ONLY JSON like {"category": "..."}. Ticket: I was charged twice for my Pro plan.”

Base · Qwen2.5-0.5B

Brilliant autocomplete

Trained only to predict the next token. It ignores the instruction, repeats or continues the text, and doesn’t stop.

Instruct · Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct

Follows the ticket

Further trained on instruction → response pairs. Returns clean JSON, but with its own label: "Billing and Service Issues", not our "Billing".

Instruction tuning gives format-following for free. It does not know your labels, scale or schema. That last gap is the job fine-tuning does.

Checkpoint 1 · Capability vs disposition

A raw, pretrained-only model is asked: “Write me a haiku about the sea.” Why might it not write one, and what does that reveal about pretraining?

Model answer

Pretraining only taught it to continue plausible text, not to follow instructions. Online, that line could be followed by anything. The model has the capability to write a haiku but not the disposition to treat your sentence as a command. Closing that gap is exactly what instruction tuning does.

3 · Motivation

Why fine-tuning exists: three reasons

1

Reliable behaviour

A prompt is a suggestion to a probabilistic system. At 10,000 replies a day, “usually follows the style” means hundreds of misses. Fine-tuning makes the behaviour the default.

2

A real skill or format gap

A strict schema, a niche citation format, a low-resource language. If the model can’t do it, no prompt will fix it. You have to teach it.

3

Cost and latency at scale

Fine-tune a small model to match a prompted giant on one narrow task: cheaper and faster on every call, forever.

Fine-tuning is for behaviour, skill, style and format. It is a clumsy, expensive way to teach facts, especially facts that change.

Three reasons: reliability of behaviour, genuine skill or format gaps, and cost and latency at scale. The lab hits all three: a strict JSON schema, consistent labels, and a 0.5B model small enough to run on every ticket.

3 · The core distinction

Three knobs, three different jobs

Fine-tuning

Changes how the model behaves

RAG

Changes what the model knows right now

Prompting

Changes what you ask in this moment

Not competitors: a sommelier’s trained taste (fine-tuning) plus tonight’s wine list (RAG) plus the guest’s request (prompt).

Use the cheapest tool that solves the problem, and combine them when you need to.

This is the single most important distinction of the day. Say it as a rule and come back to it: behaviour, knowledge, request. Most production systems combine all three.

3 · Ask in order, stop at the first yes

Five questions before you fine-tune

1. Can a better prompt fix it? It’s free and fast. Always try it first.

2. Is knowledge missing or changing? Use RAG. Never fine-tune facts that change.

3. Do you need consistent behaviour, style, format or skill, and have examples of “right”? Fine-tune. This is its home turf.

4. Is it a fixed task at high volume where cost or latency hurts? Fine-tune a smaller model.

5. More than one of the above? Combine: fine-tune for voice and format, RAG for facts.

Stop at the first yes. Question 1 honestly kills most fine-tuning requests. Our lab problem passes question 3: consistent labels and a strict format, with clear right answers.

Checkpoint 2 · Facts vs behaviour

The support bot keeps citing last year’s refund policy. Someone proposes fine-tuning it on the new policy document. Good idea? What would you do instead?

Model answer

Bad idea. A policy is knowledge that changes: fine-tuning’s worst case. You’d pay to hard-code a fact that goes stale on the next legal update, and the model may still mix up old and new. Use RAG: store the current policy, fetch the relevant section at answer time. A policy change becomes a one-document edit, not a retrain. Save fine-tuning for how the bot answers: tone, structure, refusals.

A classic trap. The policy is changing knowledge, so the answer is RAG. Contrast with the lab: our categories and JSON format are behaviour, which is why fine-tuning fits there.

4 · Stages 1–3

Define, curate, choose the chef

1

Problem definition

One sentence: the task, the input, the output, the success metric and the minimum bar. Write the evaluation before the data.

2

Data and curation

The model becomes its data. Consistency beats volume: a few hundred to a few thousand clean, on-style examples. Hold out a test set. Watch for leakage.

3

Base model selection

Size, licence, modality, language, open vs API. Pick the smallest model that clears your bar, because cost scales downstream.

In the lab: schema and targets written first · 351 balanced tickets, 35 held out · Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, the smallest model that clears the bar.

Write the evaluation before the data. Consistency beats volume. Match capacity to the task and choose the smallest model that clears the bar. The analogy strip maps each stage onto what the lab actually does.

4 · Stages 4–6

Approach, train, evaluate

4

Choose the approach

Options differ in cost by orders of magnitude. Most teams should start with LoRA or QLoRA.

5

Training

The dials are learning rate and epochs. Train conservatively. Validation loss rising while training loss falls means memorization: stop.

6

Evaluation

A held-out set and a real baseline (the prompted base model). Is it better than the cheap option, by enough to justify the cost? Plus a regression check.

In the lab: QLoRA on a T4 · loss 2.14 → 0.12, eval loss falling every epoch · baseline measured before training, then the same test re-run after.

Start with LoRA or QLoRA. Train conservatively. The overfitting signal is validation loss rising while training loss keeps falling. Evaluation always needs a baseline, and the cheapest honest baseline is the prompted base model.

4 · Stages 7–9

Deploy, monitor, iterate

7

Deployment

Behind a flag, rolled out gradually, with instant rollback. LoRA adapters are small and hot-swappable, so rollback is “detach the adapter”.

8

Monitoring and drift

Live quality, user signals, distribution drift. A model frozen in time quietly decays as the world moves.

9

Iteration

It’s a loop. Failure cases are your highest-quality new training data: add them, retrain, re-evaluate, redeploy.

In the lab: we save both a ~35 MB adapter and a merged ~1 GB model, and the misclassified tickets show exactly what data to add next.

Ship behind a flag and roll back fast; LoRA shines here. Monitor for drift. Failures are your best future training data. In the lab the priority misses on Urgent tickets are precisely that.

Checkpoint 3 · Loss is not success

“Training loss went down, so the fine-tune worked. Let’s ship.” What’s wrong with that, and which two checks do you demand?

Model answer

Falling training loss only proves the model fits the training examples better, which is also what memorization looks like. Demand: (1) evaluation on a held-out test set against the Stage-1 metric and a real baseline (the prompted base model); (2) a general-capability regression check to catch catastrophic forgetting. Loss is a training diagnostic, not a measure of success.

Low training loss can be memorization. Demand a held-out evaluation against a baseline, plus a regression check. The lab does exactly this: it scores the same 35 held-out tickets before and after training.

5 · Taxonomy · The map

Two questions sort every technique

Question 1 · How many weights move?

The cost axis

Full fine-tuning: every weight. LoRA: small add-on matrices, base frozen. QLoRA: LoRA on a 4-bit compressed base, the cheapest of all.

Question 2 · What signal teaches it?

The goal axis

SFT: examples of the right answer (input → ideal output). RLHF / DPO: preferences between two answers (A is better than B).

They combine: you usually do SFT via LoRA or QLoRA. The lab is exactly that, SFT delivered through QLoRA.

A new framing slide before the details. Students often treat LoRA and SFT as alternatives. They answer different questions: LoRA is how many weights move, SFT is what signal you train on. Every technique on the next slides sits somewhere on these two axes.

5 · Taxonomy · A

Full fine-tuning

Why

Maximum control. For deep changes of domain, language or behaviour, when you have lots of data and budget.

What

Update every weight: all billions are free to change.

How to think about it

Needs a big dataset and GPU memory of many times the model size (weights + gradients + optimizer state), plus a full model copy per task. Highest risk of catastrophic forgetting.

Most teams don’t need this. Reach for it only when LoRA has clearly hit its ceiling.

Retrain every instinct the chef has, from knife grip to plating. Total transformation, total cost.

All weights move. Most powerful, most expensive, highest forgetting risk. Reserve for deep domain shifts with ample data and budget. In the lab's memory table, full fine-tuning of even a 1.5B model doesn't fit a free T4.

Chef Qwen and the Giant Recipe Book

The premise

Chef Qwen can cook almost anything, thanks to a Giant Recipe Book with half a billion tiny notes in it. You run the Help Desk Café and need one special dish, every time: a card that says which team · how urgent · a kind reply.

The problem

Chef Qwen serves “Billing and Service Issues!” Your card only has a box called “Billing”. The chef is clever but doesn’t know your café’s rules. How do you teach them?

Idea 1: rewrite the whole book (full fine-tuning)

You’d need a library-sized room, it would take ages, and while rewriting you might rub out recipes the chef already knew. Far too much work to teach one dish.

Tell it as a story; the room will remember it longer than any formula. Map as you go: the book is the model's weights (about 500 million numbers), the special dish is our triage JSON, rewriting the book is full fine-tuning, and rubbing out old recipes is catastrophic forgetting. 25 numbers in the grid, but you only need two lists of 5 (the row and column headers) to rebuild every one of them.

LoRA’s sticky note is exactly this

Two skinny lists of numbers, A and B. Their “times table” B × A is a whole page of changes. For one page of Chef Qwen’s book: 802,816 numbers rebuilt from 28,672 (3.6%).

Rank r

How many pairs of lists you get. Bigger r means a bigger note that can describe more complicated changes. We use r = 16.

This is the "low-rank" in Low-Rank Adaptation, without the maths. A times table is a rank-1 grid: everything comes from one row list and one column list. LoRA allows r such pairs (here 16), so the note can express richer changes while staying tiny. The 802,816 versus 28,672 numbers are real: one 896 by 896 attention matrix in Qwen2.5-0.5B with r equal to 16. The notebook's story cell prints exactly these numbers.

Smart idea #2 · the Q in QLoRA

Your kitchen is tiny: make a pocket edition

The Giant Recipe Book barely fits through the door of your café’s kitchen (a free T4 GPU). So you redraw it: every number written with a fancy pen becomes the closest colour from a box of 16 crayons.

Fancy pen · 65,536 shades (16-bit)

Crayon box · 16 colours (4-bit)

4× smaller

Chef Qwen’s book: 0.99 GB → 0.25 GB. Now it fits, with room to work.

Still readable

A little less precise, like a smaller photo. NF4 puts more crayons where most numbers are.

Notes stay in fancy pen

QLoRA = pocket-edition book (4-bit, frozen) + sticky notes in full precision.

Quantization for a ten-year-old: fewer crayons, smaller book. 16-bit means 65,536 possible values per number; 4-bit means 16. Each weight snaps to the nearest of the 16 levels. NF4 places those levels where weights cluster (a bell curve), so it loses less than evenly spaced levels would. The key insight of QLoRA: the frozen book can be low precision, because the new learning lives in the sticky notes, which stay high precision.

The story in a few lines of code

A sticky note on one page

W = torch.randn(896, 896)    # the page: frozen
A = torch.randn(16, 896) * 0.01  # sticky note, part 1
B = torch.zeros(896, 16)    # part 2: starts BLANK

before = x @ W.T
after  = x @ W.T + (x @ A.T) @ B.T  # page + note

torch.allclose(before, after)  # True: blank note
W.numel(), A.numel() + B.numel()

Output

True
802,816 vs 28,672 (3.6%)

5 · Taxonomy · B · PEFT

LoRA: low-rank adaptation

Why

You don’t need to move every weight to change behaviour a lot. The change a narrow task needs is small compared with the model.

What

Freeze the base. Beside each big weight matrix, add two skinny ones, ΔW ≈ B·A, and train only those. A 10,000×10,000 change (100M numbers) becomes 10,000×8 + 8×10,000 (160K numbers).

How

Pick the rank r (8–16 for narrow tasks), the strength alpha (≈ 2×r), and which layers get adapters (attention + MLP). Trains roughly 0.1–2% of the parameters.

Don’t retrain the brain: clip targeted sticky notes onto existing skills. The chef stays intact, so forgetting is limited. The notes are small, and you can swap many sets onto one chef.

Big idea: the adaptation a task needs is low-rank. LoRA freezes the base and trains skinny matrices. The share of trainable parameters is well under 1% on big models; on our tiny 0.5B lab model it's about 2% (about 8.8M parameters), and the saved adapter is about 35 MB. Because B starts at zero, the model behaves exactly like the base at step 0, which the lab uses for a free baseline.

5 · Taxonomy · B continued

QLoRA and the adapter family

Why

Even a frozen base must sit in GPU memory. At 16 bits, big models don’t fit on one modest GPU.

What

Quantize the frozen base to 4-bit (NF4), about 4× less memory, then train LoRA adapters on top in 16-bit. The adapters absorb most of the precision loss.

How

Load with a 4-bit config, freeze, add LoRA, turn on gradient checkpointing. The pragmatic default on a single GPU, and what the lab runs on a free T4.

Adapters in general: small trainable modules inserted into a frozen network. LoRA is the most popular kind. Their value is modularity: one base, many swappable skills.

QLoRA: coach the apprentice against a compressed reference copy of the master chef. Slightly lower fidelity, far less room needed.

QLoRA = quantize the frozen base to 4-bit, then LoRA on top. It democratized fine-tuning: big models on one modest GPU. NF4 is a 4-bit format shaped like the bell curve of real weights, so it loses less than plain int4. Adapters are the general modular family.

5 · Taxonomy · C

Instruction tuning and SFT

Why

A base model has capability but not obedience: it continues text instead of following orders.

What

Supervised fine-tuning on (instruction → ideal response) pairs. SFT is the mechanism; instruction tuning is SFT aimed at following instructions in general.

How

Format each pair in the model’s chat template, train it to predict the response, and measure on held-out pairs. In the lab: 281 (ticket → triage JSON) pairs.

SFT is what you teach; LoRA or QLoRA is how you teach it cheaply. Not either/or.

Teach the chef that a ticket is a command: ticket plus the correct finished plate, repeated until “make exactly that” is second nature.

SFT is the mechanism; instruction tuning teaches obedience. You usually do SFT via LoRA, not instead of it. The lab is SFT on 281 ticket-to-JSON pairs, delivered through QLoRA.

5 · Taxonomy · D

Preference tuning: RLHF and DPO

Of two correct answers, one is more helpful, safer or on-tone. Preference tuning teaches that taste: most of what we call alignment.

RLHF

Train a judge, then optimize

People rank pairs of answers → train a reward model → use reinforcement learning to make the LLM score high. Powerful, multi-stage, somewhat unstable.

DPO

Learn straight from the pairs

The same “A is better than B” data, but optimize the model directly: no reward model, no RL. Simpler, more stable, very popular.

Refine the palate: the head chef picks the better of two good plates, thousands of times.

Between two good answers, which is better? Preference tuning teaches taste and alignment. RLHF is reward model plus RL: powerful, fiddly. DPO learns directly from preference pairs: simpler and now the common choice.

Checkpoint 4 · Pick the approach

Teach a company writing style from a few thousand examples, on one modest GPU, with instant rollback if customers hate it. Which approach, and what would you not choose?

Model answer

SFT delivered via QLoRA (or LoRA if memory allows). SFT because you have input → output style examples; QLoRA for the single-GPU budget; an adapter because it’s small and swappable, so you detach it to roll straight back. Not full fine-tuning: overkill, a full model copy per task, the most forgetting, heavy rollback. Not RLHF/DPO yet: this is a style problem with clear right answers, not a subtle preference between two good ones.

SFT via QLoRA. The adapter gives instant rollback. Not full fine-tuning (overkill); not RLHF or DPO (this is a style problem with right answers). This is exactly the recipe the lab uses.

6 · What you need

Before you start: three honest checks

Data

Hundreds, not millions

A few hundred to a few thousand examples for SFT with LoRA. Consistency beats volume: contradictions get learned faithfully.

Compute and cost

An enormous spread

Full fine-tuning: a cluster and real money. QLoRA: one GPU, from free (Colab T4) to a few hundred dollars. The approach choice is where money is saved or burned.

Team skills

Eval design is rarest

Data curation (the usual bottleneck), reading training curves, and, rarest and most important, honest evaluation design.

6 · Key risks and mitigations

Four ways fine-tuning quietly hurts you

RiskWhat happensMitigation
Catastrophic forgettingThe narrow task overwrites general skillsLoRA (frozen base), low learning rate and few epochs, mix in general data, run a regression test
OverfittingMemorizes examples; brittle on new onesStop when validation loss rises, add diversity, prefer LoRA
Data leakageTest examples bleed into training; personal data gets memorizedStrict de-duplicated splits, scrub PII, test for regurgitation
Alignment regressionSafety and tone guardrails erodeMix in safety data, red-team before and after, gate the release on it

Freezing the base with LoRA mitigates several of these at once. That is a structural choice, not a training trick.

Forgetting, overfitting, leakage, alignment regression, each with a structural mitigation. Kitchen versions: the pastry obsessive who forgot how to grill; the cook who freezes on the eleventh plate; practising only the exam dish; dropping hygiene for speed.

Checkpoint 5 · The silent risk

Name the risk where a narrow fine-tune quietly undoes general ability. Give two mitigations, at least one of them structural (about which approach you pick).

Model answer

Catastrophic forgetting. Structural: use LoRA / PEFT. With the base frozen, general skills are physically untouched. Plus any of: train conservatively (lower learning rate, fewer epochs), mix in general data, and always run a general-capability regression test so you catch forgetting before you ship.

Catastrophic forgetting. The structural mitigation is PEFT or LoRA with a frozen base, plus conservative training and a regression test. Don't let the narrow task overwrite broad competence, and measure to be sure.

7 · Economics

Where the money actually goes

Prompting

Free to build

But a recurring cost and latency on every call: long instructions × millions of calls.

RAG

Cheap to keep current

Modest build plus retrieval per call. Update a document, not a model.

Fine-tuning

Pay up front

Training, serving and retraining cost money, but every call is cheaper and faster afterwards.

Fine-tuning trades upfront and maintenance cost for cheaper per-call inference. It pays off at high volume on a stable task, and loses on low-volume or fast-changing ones.

The apprenticeship pays back only if the chef cooks that style thousands of times.

Prompting: free build, recurring per-call cost. RAG: cheap to keep current. Fine-tuning: upfront plus serving plus maintenance, but cheaper per call. In the lab, a 0.5B model with a short prompt replaces a long instruction prompt on a larger model, on every ticket.

7 · Should we fine-tune?

The go / no-go checklist

1. Prompting and RAG were tried and are genuinely not enough. (The most skipped.)

2. The problem is behaviour, style, format or skill, not changing facts.

3. You have, or can create, a few hundred clean, consistent examples.

4. The task is stable enough not to be obsolete in weeks.

5. Volume is high enough to justify the upfront and maintenance cost.

6. You can evaluate honestly: held-out set, metric, baseline.

7. You have the skills and budget for curation, training and monitoring.

8. You have a plan for the four risks.

A “no” on 1, 2 or 6 (highlighted) means you’re not ready.

You want mostly yes before committing. No on 1, 2 or 6 means not ready; that's the framework doing its job. Run the lab problem through it: a carefully prompted instruct model got only 31% category accuracy, so prompting is genuinely not enough (1 is yes); labels and format are behaviour (2 is yes); and we have a held-out test set with a baseline (6 is yes).

Every mature system blends all three tools. The skill is choosing the blend. Our lab takes the highlighted row: support-ticket classification.

The pattern everywhere: fine-tune voice, format and skill; use RAG for facts. Healthcare: never bake patient data into weights. Then segue: the highlighted support row is exactly what we build in the hands-on lab.

Lab · Problem statement

Triage every ticket, the same way, in a format software can use

Given the raw text of a support ticket, produce a category, a priority and a draft reply, as exact JSON that the ticketing system can consume with no human reformatting.

What we needWhat a general LLM does
One of our 6 categories, spelled exactly (Billing, Technical Issue, …)Invents labels: “Billing and Service Issues”
One of our 4 priorities (Low → Urgent), by our policyGuesses, with no idea what Urgent means here
Only JSON, exactly 3 keys, every single timeAdds chatty preambles, code fences, extra keys
Cheap and private enough to run on every ticketBig API models cost per call and send customer data off-site
Today a human triages by hand: slow, inconsistent between agents, and urgent tickets get buried. Downstream software can't use output that is usually right; it needs output that is reliably right in the exact format. This is a behaviour gap, so by our decision questions, it is a fine-tuning problem.

Lab · Use case

One ticket in, one JSON object out

Input: a raw ticket

Hi, I was charged $49 twice for my
Pro plan this month. Can you fix
this ASAP?

Output: exactly this shape

{"category": "Billing",
 "priority": "High",
 "suggested_reply": "Hi, I’m sorry about
  the duplicate charge…"}

category

Routes the ticket to the right team’s queue.

priority

Starts the right response-time clock.

suggested_reply

Pre-fills the agent’s reply box. A person still approves it.

Make it concrete: one ticket in, one object out. Each field has a consumer in the ticketing system. The model does the grunt work; a human still approves the reply.

Lab · Why fine-tune here

Our decision questions, applied

ApproachVerdict for ticket triage
Better promptTried: a 0.5B model follows long instructions unreliably. Category accuracy 23–31%. And every call carries the long prompt.
RAGAdds knowledge. Our gap is behaviour (format and labelling policy). There’s nothing to look up.
Big hosted modelWorks, but costs per ticket, adds latency, and sends customer data to a third party.
Fine-tune a small modelBakes format and policy into the weights: short prompts, fast, cheap, private, consistent.

Prompting and RAG change what the model knows at request time. Fine-tuning changes how it behaves every time. This is a behaviour problem.

Walk the decision questions on the real problem. Question 1 (better prompt) was tried and failed: 23–31% category accuracy in the baseline. Question 2 doesn't apply: no facts to fetch. Question 3 applies: consistent format and labels with clear right answers.

Lab · Goal and objectives

Turn a model that fails into a reliable specialist

Goal

Take a small instruct model that fails at triage and, with QLoRA on a free T4 in minutes, make it reliably emit correct, schema-valid JSON. Then prove it on tickets it never saw.

By the end you can…

  1. Explain base vs instruct, and why we start from instruct
  2. Design a dataset: schema first, balanced labels, train / val / test
  3. Format examples with the chat template, without truncating answers
  4. Load a model in 4-bit and attach LoRA, knowing every setting
  5. Run SFTTrainer within T4 memory limits
  6. Read a loss curve: healthy, overfitting or broken
  7. Evaluate before vs after with task metrics, not just loss
  8. Save, merge and run the fine-tuned model

Lab · Why QLoRA fits a free GPU

Training memory is mostly not the weights

For a 1.5B modelFull fine-tune (fp16)QLoRA (this lab)
Base weights~3 GB~1 GB (4-bit)
Gradients + optimizer state~12 GB+tens of MB (LoRA only)
Activationshighlower (gradient checkpointing)
Fits a 15 GB T4?No, too tightYes, about 6–8 GB

4-bit base: ~4× smaller

Frozen: no optimizer state for it

LoRA: gradients for ~2% of weights

Checkpointing: recompute, don’t store

Adam keeps two extra numbers per weight, so optimizer state dominates training memory. QLoRA attacks every row of the table. Our 0.5B default has even more headroom; the 1.5B numbers show you can scale up on the same free GPU. On a T4 use fp16; on Ampere or newer GPUs use bf16.

Lab · The settings that matter

Every knob, and why it’s set that way

SettingValueWhy
LoRA rank r16Enough capacity for one narrow task
lora_alpha32Strength = alpha / r = 2, the common rule
target_modulesattention + MLPBeats attention-only; the modern default
learning_rate2e-4LoRA’s sweet spot is 1e-4 to 3e-4
epochs3Small data: a few passes; watch eval loss
batch × accumulation2 × 8 = 16Big effective batch, small memory footprint
max_seq_length1024Well above the longest example: nothing truncated
precision, optimizerfp16, paged 8-bit AdamWT4 has no fast bf16; tiny optimizer memory

Out of memory? Raise accumulation, not batch size. Accumulation costs almost no memory.

Slow down here. The effective batch is per-device batch times accumulation times number of GPUs. If students hit OOM: first lower max_seq_length, then batch 1 with accumulation 16. On Ampere or newer, switch fp16 to bf16.

Lab · What the results teach

Why priority lags, and what to fix next

Learned first

Format

Valid JSON goes to 100% almost immediately. Format is the easiest habit to install.

Learned fully

Categories

Six balanced labels, about 45–50 training examples each: 100% on held-out tickets.

Still lagging

Priority

High vs Urgent is a judgement call, and only 21 of 281 training tickets were Urgent.

The next fix is more, better-balanced priority data, not more epochs. Diagnose with the data before you touch the training settings.

Lifecycle stage 9 in action: the misclassified tickets tell you exactly which examples to add.

This is the honest-evaluation payoff. Training data had High 105, Low 102, Medium 53, Urgent 21. More epochs would mostly memorize the few Urgent examples. Ask the room: how would you collect more Urgent tickets, and how would you check they're labelled consistently?

9 · Synthesis

The whole kitchen in five lines

Pretraining is culinary school: broad, instinctive, generic, frozen in time.

Prompting is the order ticket: cheapest and fastest. Always try it first.

RAG is the recipe binder: fresh and private facts without retraining.

Fine-tuning is the apprenticeship: reshapes instincts for style, format and skill. Wrong tool for changing facts.

Techniques are a cost ladder: start with SFT via QLoRA or LoRA, full fine-tuning for deep shifts, DPO or RLHF for taste.

The Monday-meeting takeaway. Judgment beats technique. Each line maps to a section of the session and to one decision in the lab.

Checkpoint 6 · Synthesis

In one line each: when do you reach for prompting, RAG and fine-tuning? And what’s the one phrase for the whole decision?

Model answer

Prompting when a better instruction can steer what the model already does. RAG when the gap is missing or changing knowledge at answer time. Fine-tuning when you need consistent behaviour, style, format or skill baked in, with clean examples and an honest evaluation.

“Fine-tune the craft, retrieve the facts, prompt the moment, and use the cheapest tool that solves the problem.”
